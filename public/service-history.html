Service History for a Modified Car

Vehicle history reports were built around dealer-reported service. Enthusiast cars are maintained almost everywhere else. This is what the reports capture, what they miss, and how to keep a record that covers the gap.

What a service history is

A service history is the dated, mileage-stamped record of everything done to keep a car running, as distinct from everything done to change it. Oil and fluids, filters, brakes, timing components, belts, bushings, the repairs. It is the boring half of a car's paperwork and it is the half that determines what the car is worth.

For an enthusiast car it does something more specific. Modifications raise the question of whether the car has been abused. A service history is the only thing that answers it, because it is a record of attention paid over time, which is the one thing that cannot be faked at the point of sale.

What the standard reports capture

Carfax, AutoCheck and their equivalents are genuinely useful, and it is worth being precise about what they do. They aggregate records that are reported to them: title and registration events from state agencies, insurance total-loss and salvage records, accident reports, odometer readings at reported events, and service entries from dealers and participating shops.

Generally capturedGenerally missed
Title, registration and ownership changesEvery modification, without exception
Reported accidents and insurance write-offsWork at independent shops that do not report
Dealer service visitsAll maintenance you performed yourself
Odometer readings at reported eventsParts fitted, brands, and part numbers
Emissions and inspection records, by stateWhy any of it was done

Read the left column carefully and you will notice what it is for: it is a risk report, designed to tell a buyer whether a car has been wrecked, stolen, or clocked. It was never designed to describe how a car has been looked after, and it does not claim to be.

Why modified cars fall into the gap

Reporting is voluntary. A shop chooses whether to participate, and many independents do not, which is not evasiveness so much as the absence of a reason to bother. And work you do yourself is never reported, because there is no business in the transaction to report it.

Now consider where an enthusiast car is actually maintained. The dealer sees it for the first few years, if at all. After that it goes to a specialist who knows the platform, or into the owner's garage on a weekend. The better the owner, the less of it shows up. A car meticulously maintained by someone who does their own work can present a thinner history report than a neglected one that happened to visit a dealer twice.

That is the gap, and nothing is going to close it from the outside. The only thing that fills it is a record the owner keeps.

What to record for each service

  • Date and mileage. Both, always. See below on why the mileage matters more than people expect.
  • What was done. Specifically. "Serviced" is not a record; "oil and filter, brake fluid flush" is.
  • Who did it. Shop name, or yourself.
  • Parts used. Brand and part number. On a modified car this is not pedantry: the correct fluid for a built engine is a different question from the correct fluid for a stock one, and the next owner will need to know which was used.
  • Cost, split between parts and labor where a shop was involved. Useful to you, and it makes an invoice legible to someone reading it later.
  • The invoice or parts receipt, attached to that entry rather than filed separately.

DIY work counts, if you record it

There is a persistent belief that self-performed maintenance is worth less to a buyer than shop work. It is not true, with one condition attached: it has to be documented, and undocumented DIY maintenance is indistinguishable from none.

Documented properly, it is often stronger evidence than a shop invoice. A generic "30,000 mile service" line tells a buyer very little. Your own entry saying which oil, which filter, at what mileage, with the receipt for the parts, tells them exactly what went into the car. The way to make that credible is boring and effective: record it the day you do it, keep the parts receipts, and take a photo. Reconstructed history reads as reconstructed, and buyers can tell.

Mileage is the spine

If you record one field beyond the date, record the mileage. Dates give you a sequence. Mileage gives you intervals, which is the thing anyone assessing the car is actually trying to work out.

A car serviced every March tells you the owner was punctual. A car serviced every 4,000 miles tells you the engine has been kept on a short interval, which for a modified engine is the difference between a healthy history and a worrying one. Mileage also travels well: it survives a car sitting for a year, and it is the only common axis between your service entries and your modification dates, so it is what lets someone line the two records up and see the car's whole life in one sequence.

Keeping it across owners

Service history is cumulative and fragile. It takes years to build and one decision to destroy, and the decision is usually somebody deciding the old paperwork is clutter.

If the car came to you with records, keep them and treat your entries as a continuation rather than a fresh start. When you sell, hand the whole thing over, including the parts you did not create. A history that stays with the previous owner is worth nothing to anyone, and an unbroken record across owners is worth real money to everyone who touches the car afterwards.

Questions

Does Carfax show modifications?

No. Vehicle history reports are built from records that get reported to them: dealer and participating shop service entries, title and registration events, insurance total-loss records, and accident reports. Modifications are not reported by anyone, so they do not appear. A history report can tell a buyer the car was not written off; it cannot tell them what has been done to it since.

Does independent shop or DIY work show up in a vehicle history report?

Often not. Reporting is voluntary, and many independent shops do not participate. Work you did yourself is never reported, because there is no business in the transaction to report it. This is the gap that matters for enthusiast cars, since that is exactly where most of their maintenance happens.

What should I record for each service?

The date, the mileage, what was done, who did it, the parts used with brand and part number, and the cost. Attach the invoice or the parts receipt to the entry itself. Mileage is the field people skip and the one that carries the most information, because it is what lets anyone later work out an interval rather than just a sequence of dates.

Does DIY maintenance count as service history?

Yes, if it is recorded. A dated entry with the mileage, the parts used, and the receipt for those parts is credible evidence, and for many enthusiast cars it is better evidence than a generic shop invoice because it says exactly which fluid and which filter went in. What does not count is a memory of having done it. Undocumented DIY maintenance is indistinguishable from no maintenance.

How far back should a service history go?

As far as you can take it. If the car had records when you bought it, keep them and treat your own entries as a continuation rather than a fresh start. An unbroken history from an earlier owner is worth real money, and it is lost permanently the first time someone decides the old paperwork is clutter.

What happens to the service record when I sell the car?

It should go with the car, because a history that stays behind is worth nothing to anyone. Hand over the full record, including the entries from before you owned it.
